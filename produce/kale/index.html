Kale: what went wrong, and what to do

Kale keeps longer than most greens when it's cold, but it yellows around ethylene and loses water quickly. Neither one makes it unsafe.

Leaves turning yellow

Ethylene gasCook it soon

What you'll seeLeaves lose their deep green color and turn yellow, often from the tips inward.

Why it happens

Kale produces very little ethylene but is sensitive to it, and exposure turns the leaves yellow. Warm storage makes it worse. 12

Is it still OK?

Safe to eat. Yellow leaves are more bitter, so cook them instead of eating them raw.

Prevent it next time
  • Keep kale away from apples and bananas
  • Store it cold, in a bag with a little air
Use it up
  • Simmer it into soups and stews
  • Blanch and freeze it for later
Where a Krisp fits

A Krisp makes a real difference here. Slows the leaves yellowing. Expect about 2–3 extra days in a closed crisper drawer. See the Double Krisp

Limp or leathery leaves

Drying outStill good

What you'll seeLeaves droop and feel soft or leathery instead of crisp.

Why it happens

Kale loses water fast. In one study, kale lost more than half its fresh weight in six days at room temperature, faster than broccoli or cabbage. 3

Is it still OK?

Still good. Soak it in cold water for 15 minutes to firm it back up for salads, or just cook it.

Prevent it next time
  • Wrap it in a damp paper towel inside a bag
  • Get it into the fridge soon after you buy it
Use it up
  • Bake it into kale chips
  • Blend it into pesto or a smoothie

How to store kale

  • Store unwashed in a plastic bag with some air
  • Wrap in a damp paper towel to maintain moisture
  • Remove any yellowed or wilted leaves
  • Can be blanched and frozen for months

Full storage details are in the Produce Storage Guide.

The part people throw away

About 28% of kale by weight is usually trimmed off as stems and ribs (USDA FoodData Central). Some of it can still be used:

  • Chop stems fine and sauté them a few minutes before the leaves
  • Blend stems into pesto or a smoothie
