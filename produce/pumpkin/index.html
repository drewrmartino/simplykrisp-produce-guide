How to store pumpkins, and why they go bad

A whole cooking pumpkin keeps a month or two in a cool, dry spot. Rot gets in through a damaged rind, and cold storage pits it.

Where to keep them
Cool, dark pantry
How long they last
1-2 months (pantry)
Best temperature
About 55°F (13°C)
Don't store next to
Apples
What are you seeing?
Soft or moldy spots on the rindSunken pits in the rindCut pumpkin turning slimy

How to store pumpkins

  • Store whole pumpkins in a cool, dry place with air moving around them
  • Do not refrigerate a whole pumpkin. Below 50°F the cold damages it
  • Keep them off bare concrete and from touching each other
  • Once cut, wrap it and refrigerate for up to 5 days, or cook and freeze the flesh

Picking good pumpkins: Hard skin a fingernail cannot dent, a firm stem still attached, and no soft spots. Pie or sugar pumpkins are the ones for cooking.

Does a Krisp help? Whole pumpkins keep in a cool, dry spot, well away from the fridge and the Krisp.

Why pumpkins go bad

Soft or moldy spots on the rind

MoldTrim and use

What you'll seeA soft, sunken or discolored patch on the rind, sometimes with fuzz.

Why it happens

USDA's storage handbook says decay is the main cause of loss in stored pumpkins, and many different fungi cause it. A nicked rind or a missing stem is where it gets in. 12

Is it still OK?

A whole pumpkin is hard and dense, so USDA's rule for firm vegetables applies: cut off a small mold spot with at least an inch around and below it. If the rot is soft and wet, has reached the seed cavity, or smells bad, toss the pumpkin.

Prevent it next time
  • Pick one with the stem attached and no cuts or soft spots
  • Keep pumpkins from touching each other
  • Check stored pumpkins every week or two
Use it up
  • Cook the trimmed pumpkin the same day

Sunken pits in the rind

Too coldTrim and use

What you'll seeSunken pits on the rind, often followed by rot once the pumpkin warms up.

Why it happens

Pumpkins get chilling injury below about 50°F. USDA's storage handbook says fridge temperatures cause sunken pits and loss of flavor, and rot follows when the pumpkin comes out of the cold. 13

Is it still OK?

Cut it open. If the flesh under the pits is firm and smells fresh, trim the pitted areas and cook it soon. Toss it if the flesh is soft, wet or moldy.

Prevent it next time
  • Keep whole pumpkins in a cool room, not the fridge
  • Bring them in from the porch before a hard frost
Use it up
  • Roast it
  • Cook, mash and freeze it

Cut pumpkin turning slimy

Bacterial rotToss it

What you'll seeThe cut surface of a stored piece turns slimy, moldy or sour-smelling.

Why it happens

Once the rind is cut, the wet flesh is exposed and spoils like any cut vegetable. 42

Is it still OK?

Toss cut pumpkin that is slimy, moldy or smells off.

Prevent it next time
  • Wrap cut pumpkin and refrigerate it
  • Use it within about 5 days, or cook and freeze it

What went wrong?

Pumpkins and ethylene

Ethylene is a gas that ripening produce gives off. In a closed crisper drawer it builds up and makes sensitive produce yellow, soften or sprout sooner.

  • Pumpkins give off only a little ethylene, so they are fine next to most other produce.
  • Pumpkins are somewhat sensitive to ethylene, so storing them away from ripening fruit helps.

Keep them apart from apples.

The part people throw away

About 30% of pumpkins by weight is usually trimmed off as seeds, strings and rind (USDA FoodData Central). Some of it can still be used:

  • Rinse the seeds and roast them with oil and salt
  • Simmer the stringy pulp into stock
