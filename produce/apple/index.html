How to store apples, and why they go bad

Apples keep for weeks in the fridge, so most of their problems are slow ones. They go soft and mealy, wrinkle as they dry out, or rot where they were bruised. They are also the biggest ethylene source in most kitchens.

Where to keep them
Fridge
How long they last
4-8 weeks (refrigerated)
Best temperature
About 32°F (0°C)
Don't store next to
Most other produce, especially lettuce, broccoli, carrots, leafy greens, avocados and bananas
What are you seeing?
Soft, mealy fleshWrinkled, leathery skinBrown, soft bruisesSoft brown rot or mold

How to store apples

  • Store in the crisper drawer in a plastic bag with holes for air circulation
  • Keep away from other produce — apples are heavy ethylene producers
  • Separate any bruised apples to prevent spoilage from spreading
  • Wash only when ready to eat

Picking good apples: A ripe apple is firm with no soft spots and has a sweet fragrance near the stem.

Does a Krisp help? Apples are the biggest ethylene source in the fridge, not a victim of it — and they out-gas any sachet. Store them apart from everything else instead.

Why apples go bad

Soft, mealy flesh

OverripeningCook it soon

What you'll seeThe flesh is soft and grainy instead of crisp and juicy.

Why it happens

Apples keep ripening after harvest, driven by their own ethylene, and they lose firmness as they go. Warmth speeds it up. UC Davis recommends storing Gala apples at about 32°F. 1

Is it still OK?

Safe to eat if there's no mold or rot. Mealy apples are better cooked.

Prevent it next time
  • Keep apples in the fridge, not in a fruit bowl
  • Buy only what you'll eat in a few weeks
Use it up
  • Cook them into applesauce or apple butter
  • Bake them into a crisp, muffins or oatmeal

Wrinkled, leathery skin

Drying outStill good

What you'll seeThe skin puckers and the apple feels lighter and slightly rubbery.

Why it happens

Apples lose water slowly through the skin. They keep best at 90 to 95 percent humidity, and an open fridge shelf is much drier than that. 1

Is it still OK?

Still good. The flesh is a little less juicy, and that's all.

Prevent it next time
  • Keep them in the crisper drawer, in a bag with a few holes in it
Use it up
  • Slice them thin and dry them into apple chips
  • Chop them into oatmeal or a salad

Brown, soft bruises

BruisingTrim and use

What you'll seeFlat brown spots under the skin that feel soft when pressed.

Why it happens

A bump breaks cells under the skin, and the damaged flesh browns. Wounds are also where decay gets in during storage. 23

Is it still OK?

Cut the bruise out and eat the rest. FDA advises cutting away damaged or bruised areas before eating.

Prevent it next time
  • Don't drop apples into the bag or the drawer
  • Eat bruised apples first
Use it up
  • Chop the good parts into a pie, a crisp or applesauce

Soft brown rot or mold

MoldToss it

What you'll seeA soft, watery brown patch that keeps growing, sometimes with blue-green or gray fuzz.

Why it happens

Blue mold and gray mold both cause decay in stored apples. Wounds are a common way in. 24

Is it still OK?

Toss an apple that is moldy or rotting. USDA notes that mold toxins are known to occur on apples, and that mold reaches deeper than the part you can see. Check the apples that were touching it.

Prevent it next time
  • Check the bag every week or so and pull out damaged apples
  • Keep bruised or cut apples apart from sound ones

What went wrong?

Apples and ethylene

Ethylene is a gas that ripening produce gives off. In a closed crisper drawer it builds up and makes sensitive produce yellow, soften or sprout sooner.

  • Apples are one of the biggest ethylene sources in the kitchen. Keep them apart from almost everything else.
  • Apples are very sensitive to ethylene. Even a little in a closed drawer speeds up how fast they age.

Keep them apart from most other produce, especially lettuce, broccoli, carrots, leafy greens, avocados and bananas.

The part people throw away

About 10% of apples by weight is usually trimmed off as cores and peels (USDA FoodData Central). Some of it can still be used:

  • Simmer cores and peels with a cinnamon stick for a quick syrup or tea
  • Make apple-scrap vinegar: cover with sugar water and let it ferment 2–4 weeks
