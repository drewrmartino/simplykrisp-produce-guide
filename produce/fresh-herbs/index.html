How to store fresh herbs, and why they go bad

Herbs aren't all the same. Basil hates the cold, parsley and mint yellow around ethylene, and cilantro rots fast if it's wet.

Where to keep them
Fridge
How long they last
5-10 days (refrigerated)
Best temperature
About 32°F (0°C)
Don't store next to
Apples, bananas and tomatoes
What are you seeing?
Basil leaves turning blackParsley, mint or cilantro turning yellowSlimy, dark leavesWilted, droopy herbs

How to store fresh herbs

  • Treat tender herbs (cilantro, parsley, basil) like flowers — trim stems and place in a glass of water
  • Cover loosely with a bag and refrigerate (except basil — keep on the counter)
  • Wrap hardy herbs (rosemary, thyme) in a damp paper towel, then seal in a bag
  • Do not wash until ready to use

Picking good fresh herbs: Bright, vibrant color with a strong aroma and no wilting or dark spots.

Does a Krisp help? In a jar on the shelf, herbs are out of the Krisp's reach. Bagged in the high-humidity drawer instead, the Krisp helps keep them from yellowing.

Why fresh herbs go bad

Basil leaves turning black

Too coldTrim and use

What you'll seeBasil leaves get dark brown or black patches, often within a day or two of going in the fridge.

Why it happens

Basil and shiso are the only common herbs that are damaged by cold, and UC Davis says to keep them above 50°F. Research has found basil leaves blacken in as little as one day at 32°F, or three days at 41°F. 12

Is it still OK?

Cold damage isn't a safety problem, but black leaves won't recover. Pick them off and use the rest today.

Prevent it next time
  • Keep basil on the counter, stems in a glass of water, out of direct sun
Use it up
  • Blend the good leaves into pesto and freeze it in an ice-cube tray

Parsley, mint or cilantro turning yellow

Ethylene gasCook it soon

What you'll seeLeaves turn yellow, stems curl, and leaves drop.

Why it happens

Ethylene causes yellowing, curling and leaf drop in herbs. UC Davis lists marjoram, mint, parsley and oregano as very sensitive. Sage, thyme, basil and rosemary barely react. 1

Is it still OK?

Safe to eat. The flavor fades as they yellow, so use them cooked, soon.

Prevent it next time
  • Keep herb jars on a different shelf from apples, bananas and tomatoes
  • Trim stems and stand tender herbs in water, loosely covered with a bag
Use it up
  • Stir them into soups, sauces or a green chimichurri

Slimy, dark leaves

Trapped moistureToss it

What you'll seeLeaves, especially cilantro, turn dark green, wet and slimy inside the bag.

Why it happens

Tender herbs rot quickly when wet leaves are trapped in a sealed bag, and mold is a common reason cilantro gets thrown out. 1

Is it still OK?

Pick out and toss the slimy sprigs. If the whole bunch smells off, toss it.

Prevent it next time
  • Don't wash herbs until you use them
  • Keep leaves dry. Only the stems should touch water

Wilted, droopy herbs

Drying outStill good

What you'll seeLeaves and stems flop but are still green.

Why it happens

Herbs lose water quickly, especially when they sit out or stay uncovered in the fridge. 1

Is it still OK?

Still good. Trim the stems and put them in cold water for 30 minutes.

Prevent it next time
  • Store tender herbs like flowers, stems in water
  • Wrap hardy herbs (rosemary, thyme) in a damp paper towel inside a bag
Use it up
  • Freeze chopped herbs in olive oil in an ice-cube tray

What went wrong?

Fresh herbs and ethylene

Ethylene is a gas that ripening produce gives off. In a closed crisper drawer it builds up and makes sensitive produce yellow, soften or sprout sooner.

  • Fresh herbs give off almost no ethylene, so they are safe to store next to most other produce.
  • Fresh herbs are very sensitive to ethylene. Even a little in a closed drawer speeds up how fast they age.

Keep them apart from apples, bananas and tomatoes.

The part people throw away

About 15% of fresh herbs by weight is usually trimmed off as stems (USDA FoodData Central). Some of it can still be used:

  • Blend soft stems (cilantro, parsley) into pesto or green sauce
  • Add tougher stems to stock, or freeze them in oil
